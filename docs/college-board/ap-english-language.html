What This Exam Validates

The AP English Language and Composition exam, administered by the College Board, evaluates students' ability to analyze rhetorical situations, assess claims and evidence, organize reasoning, apply style, and produce synthesis, rhetorical analysis, and argumentative essays. It is a paper‑based test delivered in May with multiple‑choice and free‑response sections, and the exam is scored on a 1–5 scale, with many colleges granting credit for scores of 3 or higher.

Who Should Take This Exam

High‑school students ready to read college‑level nonfiction and write clear, evidence‑based essays should take this exam. It benefits those seeking college credit or advanced placement in introductory composition courses.

Skills You Should Be Ready to Demonstrate

  • Analyze rhetorical situation
  • Evaluate claims and evidence
  • Organize reasoning
  • Apply stylistic choices
  • Synthesize sources
  • Write rhetorical analysis
  • Construct argumentative essays

How to Prepare

Use AP Classroom Progress Checks after each unit to gauge mastery of rhetorical analysis and writing skills. Practice multiple‑choice questions for speed, then write timed free‑response essays focusing on synthesis, analysis, and argument. Review sample exam questions and scoring guidelines to align your responses with AP expectations.

Domain Study Guidance

Rhetorical Situation: Study Guidance

Focuses on identifying audience, purpose, and context of a text, and how these elements shape the writer’s choices. Mastery involves recognizing rhetorical appeals and the constraints of the situation.

  • Identify audience and purpose
  • Analyze context and constraints
  • Recognize rhetorical appeals

Claims and Evidence: Study Guidance

Centers on assessing the validity and relevance of claims, and the quality of supporting evidence. Students learn to distinguish strong from weak evidence and to evaluate argument credibility.

  • Assess claim validity
  • Evaluate evidence relevance
  • Differentiate strong and weak support

Reasoning and Organization: Study Guidance

Involves analyzing how ideas are organized and how reasoning progresses throughout a text. Learners practice tracing logical flow, identifying effective organizational patterns, evaluating paragraph transitions, and assessing whether the overall structure supports the author’s purpose.

  • Trace logical flow
  • Identify organizational patterns
  • Evaluate coherence of arguments

Style: Study Guidance

Examines the writer’s stylistic choices, including diction, syntax, and rhetorical devices, and how they affect tone and audience perception. Students also consider how word choice shapes voice, how sentence variety influences rhythm, and how figurative language reinforces the argument.

  • Analyze diction and syntax
  • Identify rhetorical devices
  • Assess tone and voice

Composition: Synthesis: Study Guidance

Requires students to combine multiple sources into a cohesive essay, demonstrating synthesis of ideas and evidence while maintaining a clear thesis. They must also organize the essay with logical progression, cite sources appropriately, and ensure each paragraph contributes to the central argument.

  • Integrate multiple sources
  • Develop a unified thesis
  • Maintain logical synthesis

Composition: Rhetorical Analysis: Study Guidance

Focuses on dissecting nonfiction texts to explain how rhetorical strategies achieve specific effects, requiring close reading and analytical writing. Students practice identifying ethos, pathos, and logos, analyzing how evidence supports claims, and crafting essays that articulate the impact of these strategies on audience perception.

  • Perform close reading
  • Explain rhetorical strategies
  • Write analytical essays

Composition: Argument: Study Guidance

Involves constructing persuasive arguments supported by evidence, with attention to logical structure, style, and audience awareness. Students learn to develop a strong thesis, organize claims coherently, integrate textual evidence, and refine language for maximum persuasiveness.

  • Build persuasive thesis
  • Support arguments with evidence
  • Structure essay effectively

Exam-Day Guidance

Arrive early, bring your admission ticket and photo ID, and have a #2 pencil ready. Manage time across the multiple‑choice and free‑response sections, and review answers if time permits.

Frequently asked questions

How many sections are on the exam?

The AP English Language exam consists of two sections: a multiple‑choice portion and a free‑response portion. The multiple‑choice section tests reading and analysis, while the free‑response section includes synthesis, rhetorical analysis, and argument essays.

What is the passing score?

A score of 3 on the 1–5 AP scale is considered passing. Scores of 3, 4, or 5 are typically accepted for college credit or placement by most institutions.

How long is the exam?

The exam lasts 195 minutes, administered in a single day during May. It is a paper‑based test that includes both multiple‑choice and free‑response items officially.

How much does the AP English Lang exam cost?

The standard fee is $98 per exam. Students who qualify for fee reductions pay $55, and an additional $40 international surcharge applies for candidates outside the United States.
